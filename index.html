Introduction

- Forest fires can reach temperatures up to a 1000 degrees, an environment typically unliveable for most organisms.

- However, the black fire beetle, or melanophila acuminata, has been observed to approach these fires and use the lack of the burnt trees’ defence mechanisms to mate and lay their eggs.

- The mechanism by which these beetles find and approach these fires is through the detection of infrared radiation.

- They have specialised sensory pit organs which convert the infrared radiation from the fires to heat, leading to a series of mechanical changes within the receptor, allowing bioelectric nerve transmission.

- This highly sensitive and extremely rapid response could allow the development of invaluable sensors for human use.

Methods

01 Infrared Absorption & Energy Conversion

The beetle's infrared sensory organs detect IR radiation (3 µm & 10 µm) via specialized sensilla. The cuticular dome absorbs IR, heating the liquid inside the microfluidic chamber to trigger detection.

02 Liquid Expansion & Pressure Increase

IR-induced heating expands the liquid in the sealed microfluidic core, but the rigid cuticular shell restricts it, increasing internal pressure, which is denoted above.

03 Membrane Deformation

Increased pressure deforms a flexible membrane, stretching mechanosensitive ion channels in the neuron's dendritic tip, similar to hair cell activation in the ear.

04 Neural Activation & Fire Detection

Membrane deformation opens ion channels (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺), depolarizing the neuron and generating an action potential. The beetle processes the signal and navigates toward the thermal source, including the forest fire.

Results

01 Sensory Organ Structures

Each sensory pit in the bettle contains 50–100 sensilla. These sensilla are not just thermal sensors, they use a protein-based photomechanical mechanism for IR detection.

02 Performance of Different Materials

Gases such as CO₂ significantly increase the deformation amplitude of the membrane, making it more suitable for biomimetic sensors than water.

03 An Uncooled MEMS-based IR Sensor

The design includes: a pressure chamber (mimic the sensillum’s response to IR-induced expansion), compensation mechanism: (prevent large temperature fluctuations from affecting sensitivity) bubble-free filling method (ensures reliable performance)

The MEMS-based sensor achieved a sensitivity of ~ 1 W/m², closely matching beetle behavioral experiments.

Applications

Early wildfire detection

Detect small temperature increases before fires spread

Integrated with AI fire prediction models for automated alerts

Industrial fire safety

Detect overheating machinery by detecting IR before flames appear

Integrated into smart safety systems to trigger auto-shutdowns

Military and surveillance

Detect enemy heat signatures, missile launches or thermal activity

Environmental monitoring

Can detect climate change by detecting heat variations in various ecosystems

Current Challenges

Scalability and cost

Current MEMS based sensors are expensive to produce

Environmental factors

Harsh outdoor environments can degrade sensor performance

Interferance from background sources

Refinement needed to distinguish wildfires from other heat sources

Future Work

Smart firefighting technologies

AI driven firefighting sensors can be used to analyse fire behaviour and predict fire paths

Integration within sattellites and drones

MEMS-based sensors in satellites can enable global wildfire tracking

Space exploration

Future missions could use these sensors to detect heat sources and prevent equipment malfunctions
